# Pre-Proposal: Colonization Resistance Against Fungal Pathogens - A Synthetic Microbiome Approach

## Background and Significance

Invasive fungal infections cause >1.5 million deaths annually, with mortality rates exceeding 50% for some infections. *Candida*, *Aspergillus*, and *Cryptococcus* species are leading causes. A major risk factor is disruption of the commensal microbiome, particularly by broad-spectrum antibiotics. The healthy microbiome provides "colonization resistance" against pathogens, but mechanisms protecting against fungal pathogens remain poorly defined. Understanding these mechanisms could enable microbiome-based strategies to prevent deadly fungal infections in high-risk populations (immunocompromised patients, ICU patients, individuals on prolonged antibiotics).

## Knowledge Gap

While bacterial colonization resistance against bacterial pathogens has been extensively studied, colonization resistance against fungi is understudied. Key unknowns include:

1. Which commensal bacteria provide colonization resistance against specific fungal pathogens?
2. What are the molecular mechanisms? (nutrient competition, direct inhibition, immune modulation)
3. How do bacteria-fungus-host interactions integrate to determine infection outcomes?
4. Can minimal bacterial consortia recapitulate full microbiome colonization resistance?
5. How do clinical antibiotic regimens compromise anti-fungal colonization resistance?

## Proposed Research

### Specific Aims

**Aim 1: Identify bacteria that confer colonization resistance against fungal pathogens**
- Screen commensal bacterial isolates for anti-fungal activity
- Build synthetic communities with varying colonization resistance capacity
- Identify minimal consortia that prevent fungal pathogen establishment

**Aim 2: Define molecular mechanisms of bacterial-mediated colonization resistance**
- Test alternative mechanisms: nutrient competition, direct inhibition via antimicrobials, modulation of host immunity
- Use metabolomics to identify bacterial metabolites inhibiting fungal growth or virulence
- Employ genetic manipulation to validate mechanisms

**Aim 3: Translate findings to in vivo models and define restoration strategies**
- Test protective bacterial consortia in gnotobiotic mouse models of fungal infection
- Assess whether consortia can restore colonization resistance after antibiotic treatment
- Optimize consortium composition for clinical translation

## Approach and Methods

**Bacterial Strain Library**: Assemble collection of >100 human gut commensal isolates representing major phyla. Prioritize strains from individuals resistant to *Candida* colonization.

**Fungal Pathogens**: Focus on *Candida albicans*, *Candida auris* (emerging multi-drug resistant pathogen), and *Aspergillus fumigatus*.

**High-Throughput Screening**:
- Co-culture individual bacterial strains with fungi in microwell plates
- Measure fungal growth, morphology, and biofilm formation
- Screen culture supernatants for soluble inhibitory factors

**Synthetic Community Assembly**:
- Combine protective strains into synthetic communities
- Test communities across range of compositions and ratios
- Use design-of-experiment approaches to optimize community configuration

**Mechanism Elucidation**:
- **Nutrient competition**: Use metabolomics to identify depleted nutrients; add back nutrients to test if resistance is lost
- **Direct inhibition**: Fractionate culture supernatants; identify antimicrobial molecules via LC-MS/MS; synthesize and test candidates
- **Immune modulation**: Use intestinal epithelial cell and immune cell co-cultures; measure cytokine production, antimicrobial peptide expression

**Genetic Validation**: Use CRISPR and transposon mutagenesis to delete candidate genes; test whether loss-of-function mutants lose protective capacity.

**In Vivo Validation**: 
- Gnotobiotic mice colonized with synthetic communities
- Challenge with fungal pathogens (oral gavage for *Candida*, intranasal for *Aspergillus*)
- Measure fungal burden, tissue invasion, survival
- Compare antibiotic-treated vs. consortium-restored mice

**Clinical Relevance**: Test effects of common antibiotic regimens on protective consortia; design restoration strategies compatible with ongoing antibiotic therapy.

## Expected Outcomes and Impact

This research will:
1. Identify bacterial species and minimal consortia that prevent fungal pathogen colonization
2. Define molecular mechanisms of anti-fungal colonization resistance
3. Develop rationally-designed bacterial consortia as "living prophylactics" for high-risk patients
4. Establish experimental framework for studying bacteria-fungus-host interactions
5. Inform antibiotic stewardship by revealing impacts on anti-fungal resistance

Successful translation could transform management of patients at risk for invasive fungal infections. Rather than prophylactic antifungals (which drive resistance and have toxicity), patients could receive protective bacterial consortia. This addresses a critical unmet medical need.

## Innovation

- Systematic, mechanistic dissection of anti-fungal colonization resistance using synthetic communities
- Integration of bacterial, fungal, and host perspectives
- Focus on clinically important, emerging pathogens (*C. auris*)
- Rational design of therapeutic bacterial consortia based on mechanistic understanding
- Assessment of antibiotic impacts on anti-fungal resistance - a clinically important but understudied area

## Feasibility

Our lab has extensive experience with gut bacterial culture and synthetic communities. We have established collaborations with clinical mycologists and access to gnotobiotic mouse facilities. Preliminary data show that spent medium from *Bacteroides* species inhibits *C. albicans* hyphal formation, a key virulence determinant. Strain collections are available through collaborators and repositories (BEI Resources).

## Translational Pathway

We will work with infectious disease clinicians to identify target populations (hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, solid organ transplant recipients, ICU patients). Regulatory strategy will follow live biotherapeutic product (LBP) pathway established by FDA. Industry partnerships will support scale-up and clinical trials.

## Conclusion

Fungal infections represent a major, growing threat to human health. Microbiome-based prevention strategies could reduce mortality and antifungal drug use. This research will generate foundational knowledge and translational tools to address this critical need.
